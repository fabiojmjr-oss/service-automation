# Eight findings, for the person who signs the business case

*[Português](FINDINGS.pt-BR.md)*

The [root README](../README.md) states each finding next to the arithmetic that produced it. This
document does the other job: it says, for each of the eight, **what decision the finding lands on, what
a competent operation would have decided without it, and what to do instead.** No new figure appears
here. Every number below is quoted from the README, which is itself under test — a figure that moved
breaks the build before it reaches this page.

One warning before the list. These are findings about **one declared synthetic account**, generated by
`svclab.synth` from written-down parameters. They are not market statistics and they are not a
benchmark. What transfers is the **method** — and, in four of the eight cases, a **closed form** that
holds on any account, not only on this one.

| # | The decision it lands on | The figure that decides it |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Which number to judge an automation on | Containment ranks the four policies in the exact reverse of resolution |
| 2 | Whether the quality score may be used as evidence | The panel reports 69.45% of any real difference, exactly |
| 3 | Three defaults nobody was asked to approve | The promised headcount is reachable if 29.23% of customers give up |
| 4 | Whether a customer is a person or a row | A correlation of 0.2500 between people becomes 0.0448 in the outcome |
| 5 | Who the volume actually comes from | Heavy users generate 58.1% of contacts and 61.2% of the hours |
| 6 | What an unresolved contact costs after today | The tail is 1.0448 sessions, and the lever is not the customer |
| 7 | Which constraint decided the headcount | An occupancy ceiling alone is satisfied by understaffing |
| 8 | The difference between an agent count and a payroll | Eleven agents cost twelve people, by fixed point |

## 1. The KPI ranks the policies backwards

**The decision.** Which single number an automation programme is steered by, and therefore which policy
gets funded.

**What the case said.** Containment, the standard metric, at 0.8169 for the most aggressive bot policy.
On that number the programme is a success and the policy is the winner.

**What the account says.** The same policy resolves **0.4476** of contacts against **0.7271** for the
policy containment ranks last. The containment ranking is the exact reverse of the resolution ranking,
and all four defensible containment definitions — including the strictest — prefer the same wrong
policy. **28%** of the quoted containment never reached the queue, measured against customers who never
met the bot. Total conversations rose **26.6%**, because a contained contact that comes back was a
deferred contact. The residue left for humans is **1.58** times harder than what the bot resolved, and
**21.98%** of what the bot resolved would have resolved itself.

The headcount case inherits all of it. It promised **8.49** agents of saving against a baseline of 14;
the queue gives back **3**, an overstatement of **2.83** times. And **1.49** agents of that gap is pure
Erlang non-linearity — the same reduced volume at the same handling time, with no behavioural assumption
in it at all.

**What to do instead.** Steer on resolution and on the total conversation count, and require a
containment figure to name which of the four definitions it is. Never present a headcount saving derived
by multiplying a queue by a proportion: a queue is Erlang, and the error is not conservative.

## 2. The replacement meter is an unqualified gauge

**The decision.** Whether the quality panel's pass rate may be used as evidence in a comparison of two
policies — which is exactly what finding 1 asks it to do.

**What the case said.** Nothing. A quality rubric is treated as a reading, not as an instrument, and
gets qualified roughly never.

**What the account says.** Reading the same session twice, each grader contradicts their earlier verdict
on **17%** to **23%** of them, at a kappa between graders of **0.54** to **0.57**. The pass rate on
identical sessions runs from **0.3815** to **0.5683** depending on whose week it was — a factor of
**1.49** on the headline. And measurement error does not add noise to a comparison, it **shrinks** it,
by a closed form: a binary assessor multiplies any difference by the Youden index. This panel's index is
**0.6945**, so a true gap of 0.5459 is reported as **0.3791** — **69.45%** of the real difference,
exactly rather than approximately. A gauge whose sensitivity and specificity sum to one reports exactly
zero however large the truth; below that it reverses the sign. The bill arrives in sample size:
**10.07** sessions per arm with a perfect gauge, **25.03** with this one.

The automated judge agrees with the declared standard **0.8850** of the time against the best human
grader's **0.8675** — and validated against a single grader it scores anywhere from 0.5450 to 0.7121 of
kappa. The counterweight belongs in the same breath: the panel as a **committee** beats the judge,
**0.7979** to **0.7826**.

**What to do instead.** Run the gauge study before the comparison, not after the result is disputed.
Report the Youden index alongside every quality delta, and size the test on the attenuated difference.
Treat "agreement with our reviewers" as a measurement of the reviewers.

## 3. Three defaults nobody was asked to approve

**The decision.** Where to cut the router's score, whether impatient customers exist, and how big a
policy test has to be. All three were settled by default and none was priced.

**What the case said.** Tune the classifier on accuracy; staff to a service level; compare two policies
on the contacts that happened.

**What the account says.** Tuning on accuracy rather than on cost costs **7.51** human hours a month —
the accuracy-maximising cut is 0.45 and the cost-minimising one 0.48, three hundredths. One threshold
per intent instead of one for all five saves a further **11.5552** seconds per contact, **102.1** hours
over the same month. And the textbook closed form for that cut is **12.5%** worse than the single number
it was meant to improve on, because it assumes a calibrated probability and this score is a margin.

On the queue: the promised headcount is reachable, if **29.23%** of customers give up. Erlang C has
nothing to say below eight agents at this load; Erlang A does, and at six agents the queue is stable
with 29.23% abandonment and the rest at **89.45%** occupancy. The business case's 5.51 agents was never
impossible — it was an unstated decision to answer seven contacts in ten. Worse, an understaffed queue
manufactures its own work: abandoned contacts return, so load settles at a fixed point **21.1%** above
base and abandonment rises to **38.45%**. At eleven agents the identical feedback adds 2.0%.

On the test: customers repeat, so a per-contact test that believes it runs at five per cent is really
running at **8.43%**, and detecting the policy gap needs **523** contacts per arm rather than **405**.

**What to do instead.** Tune thresholds on the cost of the two mistakes, per intent, and calibrate
before applying any closed form. Never quote a service level from a model without impatience. Size
policy tests with the design effect in them.

## 4. A correlation between people is not a correlation between outcomes

**The decision.** Whether the clustering warning in finding 3 is worth acting on, and which correlation
to estimate when sizing a test.

**What the case said.** Either that customers are independent, or — once warned — that the correlation
between their traits is the number to plug in.

**What the account says.** The account is built twice from the identical noise, so the independent world
is a **control** rather than a baseline. Nothing already published moves: the largest relative movement
across eight published metrics is **0.89%**. That is the precondition, not the result — it is what makes
everything else a statement about dependence and nothing else.

A correlation declared at **0.2500** between customers measures 0.2535 on the latent scale, 0.2463 on
the difficulty itself, and **0.0448** on the resolution a test is actually run on — a factor of **5.6**.
A resolution is a coin whose bias is correlated, not a correlated coin. So the measured design effect is
**1.0693**, a nominal 5% test really runs at **5.80%**, and power at the old sizing is **0.7759** rather
than 0.8026: **2.7 points of power, not 11.**

Two consequences worth carrying into any dashboard. The KPI is blind to what the operation feels: the
share of two-contact customers failed **both** times rises from 0.1219 to **0.1406** — 98 more people —
while the resolution rate moves by 0.0002, because a rate is contact-weighted and a complaint is
customer-weighted. And the correction most analysts reach for, averaging each customer's average, costs
**13.3%** of standard error **in the independent world where there is nothing to correct**, against the
correlation's own contribution of **1.1%**.

**What to do instead.** Estimate the correlation of the outcome you are testing, never of the trait you
believe drives it. Report a customer-weighted failure measure beside the contact-weighted rate. Keep a
control world rather than a better story.

## 5. The volume comes from people the average hides

**The decision.** Who the operation is actually serving, and therefore where a fix is worth deploying.

**What the case said.** A mean of contacts per customer, and an implicit assumption that the busiest
customers are busy by chance.

**What the account says.** The identical contacts regrouped into a realistic rate distribution, with the
heavy users correlated to the difficult ones, keep **every per-contact figure identical bit for bit**.
Mean contacts per customer rise 23%. The **effective** cluster size — the size-weighted mean, which is
the quantity a design effect is computed from — rises **78%**, from **3.23** to **5.75**. Concentration
is a statement about the variance of the cluster sizes, and the mean barely registers it.

Which brings the alarm back: the design effect is **1.2246**, and a nominal 5% test here really runs at
**7.65%**. Cost concentrates faster than volume: heavy users are 58.1% of the contacts but **61.2%** of
the hours, a separation of **3.1** points where the equal-rate control shows 0.3 — heavy users do not
merely contact more, each contact costs more. The resolution rate falls **1.53** points with no change
of policy at all, because mean difficulty per contact rises **9.9%** when difficult customers send more
contacts each. And wave 1's central estimate loses a third of its precision: **+66%** relative error on
the same contacts, same arms, same bot.

**What to do instead.** Segment by contact frequency before costing any fix, and report hours by segment
rather than volume by segment. Use the size-weighted cluster mean in any design effect. Treat every
per-customer metric as conditional on a definition of "customer" that must be stated.

## 6. The tail is short here, and that is the finding

**The decision.** How much future work an unresolved contact commits the operation to, and which lever
reduces it.

**What the case said.** That a contained contact is closed. Every earlier wave allowed exactly one
return and named it as the reason its figures understated the queue.

**What the account says.** Run the chain to its declared end and **720** contacts need a third attempt,
seven a fourth: **4.88%** more human hours, eventual resolution from 0.8067 to **0.8291**. Small — and
the closed form says why. A contact reopens only if the customer returns **and** the human failed again,
so the sessions one unresolved contact generates are a geometric series in the **product**. Here that
product is **0.0429** and the series is **1.0448** sessions however many attempts are allowed.
Truncating at one return cost **0.18%**; at a reopen rate of 0.5 it would have cost a third.

So the geometric tail is not a property of customers who return — it is a property of an operation that
keeps failing them, and the lever is the human's first-contact resolution, not the customer's return
rate. The chain also charges the policy containment rewarded most: extra hours of **5.74%** against
3.86% for the human queue. And it produces a **third** ranking, the steepest yet — days to resolution
**1.31** against **0.16**, with **40.5%** of the aggressive policy's eventual resolutions arriving on a
later attempt.

**What to do instead.** Measure the reopen rate first, then decide whether a tail model is worth
building: at the rate this account has, one return captures the queue to within 0.18%, and at 0.5 it
misses a third. Put days to resolution beside the rate — a rate has no time in it, and three days is not
a rounding error, it is the complaint.

## 7. Name the constraint that decided the headcount

**The decision.** How a staffing number is produced: by hitting one target and reporting the rest, or by
declaring every ceiling and saying which one bound.

**What the case said.** Staff to a service level. Occupancy and abandonment come out as outputs — which
is how a queue got called "perfectly stable" at six agents while a third of its customers walked away.

**What the account says.** An occupancy ceiling pursued alone is satisfied by **understaffing**: eight
agents hold occupancy at **0.8121** while answering **17.51%** inside the target and losing **14.34%**
of customers, because the customers who abandon are what keeps the occupancy down. A humane-sounding
target rewards a queue for losing people. The service level and the abandonment ceiling reach the same
eleven agents by independent routes and bind together. Six agents fails all three: **stability is not a
plan.** The napkin — load divided by the occupancy ceiling — gives 9 where the queue needs 11, wave 1's
error in a new costume.

Scale cuts both ways. Agents per erlang falls from **3.00** at one erlang to **1.18** at four hundred, a
**61%** drop, which is the case for consolidation. But the binding constraint moves from the service
level to the occupancy ceiling at about 45 erlangs: past there the queue is limited by the agent's
tolerance rather than the customer's, and those two are negotiated with different people. And at the
chosen plan the thinnest margin is **+0.0176** on abandonment — "all constraints met" and "one absence
from breaching" are the same sentence.

**What to do instead.** Write the ceilings down before computing a headcount, and publish which one
bound plus the margin on each. Never present an occupancy target without a service level and an
abandonment ceiling beside it.

## 8. An agent count is not a payroll

**The decision.** How many people to fund, as distinct from how many agents the queue needs — and
whether loosening an occupancy target pays for itself.

**What the case said.** A headcount, plus a buffer somebody chose.

**What the account says.** The premium is not a buffer, it is the solution of a loop no staffing model
contains: occupancy raises attrition, attrition empties seats, an empty seat is not an agent, fewer
agents raise occupancy. Eleven agents cost twelve people; at a hundred erlangs **119** agents cost
**130**, with **7.13** seats empty and **9.51** people ramping at any moment. The premium is **not
monotone** in queue size, because integer arithmetic dominates a small queue and attrition a large one.

Which prices the efficiency finding 7 celebrated: the large queue needing only 1.18 agents per erlang
loses **36.1%** of its staff a year and hires 57 people to stand still. The exchange rate is **1.01** —
twenty-seven more people buys **27.39** fewer hires a year. Read in the repository's own currency, a
departure wastes **2.30** person-months, so an extra agent costs 12 person-months a year and returns
2.33: a ratio of **0.19**. On the queue's own books, loosening occupancy loses by a factor of five —
which does not make it wrong. It means the case rests entirely on what a departure costs **outside** the
queue, and naming that missing number is the deliverable.

Two boundaries. On the small queue there is no trade at all — every ceiling from 0.90 down to 0.70 needs
the same twelve people, and the rate comes back as no-number rather than as zero, because zero would say
the trade was free. And the loop does not run away: the attrition slope has to be **12.5** times steeper
before the plan's payroll collapses, though only five times steeper two people short — **robustness is a
property of the payroll, not only of the curve.**

**What to do instead.** Fund a payroll, not an agent count, and show the fixed point rather than a
margin. Before arguing for a lower occupancy ceiling, get the cost of a departure outside the queue
named by whoever owns it — on the queue's own books that argument loses. And read the spiral question as
a funding question: the plan is robust, two people short of it is not.

## What none of this says

It does not say containment is useless — it says containment cannot rank resolution, which is a narrower
and harder claim. It does not say the automation was a mistake: the queue still gives back three agents,
and three agents is real. It says the case that promised 8.49 was wrong by a factor, in three
identifiable places, each of which is measurable before the money is committed.

And it says nothing about anyone's real operation. There is no employer, client, customer, vendor or
platform data anywhere in this repository, and no language model in it either — see
[`DISCLAIMER.md`](../DISCLAIMER.md) for both, and [`ROADMAP.md`](ROADMAP.md) for what is deliberately
absent, what is still open, and the defects recorded rather than quietly fixed.
